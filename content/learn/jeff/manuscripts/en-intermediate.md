# Jeff — Episode 1: Arrival

Jeff's phone buzzed again.

**I've got my bags. Where are you?**

He replied: **Near the arrivals exit.**

**There are about six doors here.**

**Just choose one. I'll find you.**

**Very funny.**

Jeff put his phone away, but another message arrived before he could finish zipping his jacket.

**How am I supposed to know which door to use?**

He was writing a reply when his friend came through the doors, pulling a suitcase with one hand and holding his phone in the other.

Jeff called out and waved. His friend saw him, and they managed to hug with the suitcase in the way.

“Man, I thought I'd never get out of there.”

“Well, you made it.”

“Barely.”

Jeff stepped back. His friend had a backpack over one shoulder and was holding the suitcase handle almost against his chest.

“Did you bring everything you own?”

“More or less. I'm moving here, remember?”

“You're staying with me for the first two nights.”

“Yeah, yeah. Then I get a real apartment.”

They walked farther into the terminal. After a few steps, his friend slowed down to look around.

“Is it just me, or does Japan smell different?”

“Different how?”

“I don't know. It just does.”

Jeff didn't notice any unusual smell.

The suitcase rattled across the smooth floor as they passed a row of shops.

“This airport is incredibly clean.”

“Are you hungry?”

“Starving. They gave us this tiny thing on the plane. I still don't know what it was.”

“Come on. There's a place to eat here.”

They found a restaurant in the terminal and left the suitcase beside their table. His friend looked through a menu full of pictures.

“All right,” he said. “I've been practicing my Japanese. I'll order.”

“You sure?”

“I've got this.”

He raised a hand to get the server's attention.

“Sumimasen!”

The server came over. His friend opened his mouth.

“Uh...”

He checked the menu again.

“Um...”

Jeff waited.

“Okay. You order.”

Jeff pointed at a photograph.

「かつ丼、二つお願いします」

The server repeated the order, and Jeff nodded.

Once the server had left, his friend leaned across the table.

“What did you say?”

“Two katsudon.”

“That's it?”

“That's it.”

“I knew how to say the first part.”

“You did great with the first part.”

“Shut up.”

His friend went back to the menu.

“So what did you order for me?”

“Fried pork cutlet over rice, with egg on top.”

“Sounds good.”

“I think you'll like it.”

When the bowls arrived, his friend leaned forward to look. Strips of pork lay beneath soft egg, and some of the sauce had soaked into the rice.

He took a bite and kept chewing while Jeff pulled apart his wooden chopsticks.

“Oh, that's good.”

“Yeah?”

“Really good. What's the sweet stuff?”

“The sauce.”

“I can taste that. What's in it?”

“I'm not exactly sure.”

“You've been here three months.”

“That doesn't mean I know every sauce in Japan.”

Jeff was only halfway through his bowl when his friend finished his.

“You eat fast.”

“I told you I was starving.”

His friend glanced at what was left in Jeff's bowl.

“Can we get another one?”

“Another what?”

“That.”

“You just ate one.”

“And I liked it.”

By the time Jeff finished, his friend was studying the menu again.

“Come on. One more.”

Jeff had never eaten two bowls of katsudon before. He looked at his empty bowl and called the server back.

「すみません。かつ丼、もう二つお願いします」

The second bowls arrived. Jeff ate more slowly this time. His friend started straight away but put down his chopsticks halfway through.

“You okay?” Jeff asked.

“Yeah. Just taking a break.”

“You asked for another one.”

“I remember.”

They finished a few minutes later. His friend leaned back and didn't move.

“I might have made a mistake.”

“You think?”

“How are you feeling?”

“About the same.”

They paid and left the restaurant. His friend went straight to a bench along the concourse, and Jeff sat beside him.

For a while, they watched travelers pass with their suitcases and waited for their stomachs to settle.

“Do you always eat two bowls?”

“No.”

“Seriously?”

“First time.”

“Then why did you order two?”

“You wanted another one.”

“I meant one for me.”

Jeff turned toward him.

“You could have said that.”

His friend laughed, then stopped and held his stomach.

“Don't.”

“Okay. How far is your place?”

“We've still got a train ride.”

“Could we take a cab?”

Jeff looked from the suitcase to his friend.

“From here? You're paying, right?”

His friend sank back against the bench.

“Train it is.”

# Jeff — Episode 2: The Apartment

By the time they got to the train, neither of them wanted to talk about food anymore. Jeff's friend stood near the doors with one hand on his suitcase.

They found seats, but getting the suitcase between them was a struggle. Each time they turned it, one of the wheels got stuck.

“Here, give me your backpack,” Jeff said.

“I've got it.”

“I can see that. Give me the backpack.”

Jeff took it onto his lap while his friend squeezed the suitcase between his knees.

The train started moving. Across the aisle, several passengers had their eyes closed. His friend leaned toward him.

“How long are we on this thing?”

“A while.”

“That's not a time.”

“I'll tell you when we have to get off.”

They changed trains. At the next station, his friend almost lost his grip on the suitcase while lifting it across the gap.

“This is ridiculous. How far out do you live?”

A few passengers turned to look.

“Keep your voice down,” Jeff said.

“What?”

“People are sleeping.”

His friend looked along the car, then turned toward the window.

They passed apartment buildings and shopping streets. A route map above the doors caught his friend's eye.

“Wait. We're in Kanagawa?”

“Yeah.”

“I thought you lived in Tokyo.”

“I do.”

“Then why are we in Kanagawa?”

“The train goes through Kanagawa, then back into Tokyo.”

His friend studied the map again.

“That's a lot of trouble just to get home.”

“It's not usually this much trouble.”

“You don't usually have me and a suitcase.”

“Exactly.”

They finally got off at Aihara. The station looked tiny compared with the ones they'd passed through. Jeff led him past a row of parked bicycles and down a quiet street.

“This is still Tokyo?”

“Yep.”

“You sure?”

“Pretty sure.”

His friend caught up, the suitcase wheels rattling behind him.

A few minutes later, they reached Jeff's apartment building. His friend stopped at the bottom of the stairs.

“This is it?”

“This is it.”

“It's old.”

“I know.”

“How much do you pay for a place like this?”

“Not much. My uncle owns it.”

“Your uncle?”

“He lives over in Sagamihara. He lets me stay here cheap.”

His friend grabbed the railing and started dragging the suitcase up the stairs.

“Does your uncle have an elevator somewhere?”

“Nope.”

At the apartment door, Jeff took off his shoes and stepped up into the room. His friend rolled the suitcase across the doorway and started to follow it.

“Shoes,” Jeff said.

“What about them?”

“Take them off.”

His friend glanced at Jeff's socks.

“Right here?”

“Yeah.”

“But I'm not even inside yet.”

“You're in enough.”

He stepped back and pulled off one sneaker while balancing on the other foot. When his bare foot landed on the lower floor by the door, Jeff pointed farther into the room.

“Up here.”

“I'm trying.”

He took off the second shoe, stepped into the room, and pulled the suitcase in after him.

“What about the wheels?” he asked.

Jeff looked at the suitcase.

“Just leave it here for now.”

The room was so small they had to step around each other to move. A low table stood near the center, and a folded futon rested against the wall. The kitchenette was only a few steps from the suitcase.

His friend turned slowly, taking it all in.

“Where's the bedroom?”

Jeff pointed at the floor.

“You're standing in it.”

“And the living room?”

“Same place.”

“Dining room?”

“You're getting it.”

His friend looked up at the ceiling.

“I'm only here for two nights, right?”

“That's what you told me.”

“Just checking.”

They moved the low table to make space for a second futon. While Jeff was shifting it, his friend noticed two folders beside a shelf.

“What's all this?”

“Nothing. Put the table over there.”

But his friend had already opened one. Inside were the flattened fronts of instant ramen packages, kept in clear plastic sleeves.

“Dude. Have you eaten anything besides ramen?”

“You just watched me eat two bowls of katsudon.”

“Oh, right.”

He turned another page. The packages came in different colors, with pictures of bowls, noodles, and cartoon characters.

“Why are you keeping the bags?”

“Just the fronts. They're all different.”

“How many do you have?”

“About seventy.”

“Seventy? You've only been here three months.”

“There are a lot of kinds.”

His friend flipped through a few more pages, then opened the other folder. It contained clean paper sleeves from disposable chopsticks, some plain and some printed with restaurant names.

“You collect these too?”

“Yeah.”

“They're just wrappers. You're supposed to throw them away.”

“Some have different designs.”

“So you came all the way to Japan to collect things Japanese people throw in the trash.”

Jeff held out his hand for the folder.

“You done?”

“I'm just saying.”

“I know what you're saying.”

His friend handed it back. Jeff put both folders on the shelf, farther from the suitcase this time.

They spread out the two futons. There was enough room to walk between them and the table, as long as they turned sideways.

His friend sat on the edge of one and tried to stretch his legs.

“What do you do in here all day?”

“I'm usually at school or work.”

“I still can't picture you working at a restaurant.”

“Neither can I.”

“What about when you're home?”

“Sleep. Eat. Sometimes go out.”

His friend glanced toward the shelf.

“Collect trash.”

Jeff tossed a pillow at him.

“You can sleep on the floor without one, you know.”

He caught it and tucked it behind his back.

“Okay, okay.”

A moment later, he stood up.

“Where's the bathroom?”

Jeff pointed toward the narrow door beside the kitchenette.

“In there.”

The door closed. Jeff was pulling a sheet over his futon when his friend called from the bathroom.

“Jeff!”

“What?”

“What the hell? The toilet's in the floor!”

# Jeff — Episode 3: A Day at Takao

Jeff woke up at six. His friend was still asleep on the other futon, with one foot sticking out from under the blanket.

He got dressed and checked the train schedule. At seven, he leaned over and gave his friend's shoulder a nudge.

“Hey. Get up.”

His friend pulled the blanket over his head.

“No.”

“It's seven.”

“I got here yesterday.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you waking me up?”

“We're going to Mount Takao.”

“What's that?”

“A mountain. There's a chairlift.”

He lowered the blanket.

“You should've said that first.”

He sat up, checked the clock, then dropped back onto the futon.

“Five more minutes.”

Jeff went to put the kettle on.

His friend stopped on the way to the bathroom.

“Hey. That toilet. Are they all like that?”

“No, mostly just older places.”

“How did you figure out how to use it?”

“I asked my uncle.”

“You asked your uncle how to use a toilet?”

“You saw it.”

His friend glanced toward the bathroom.

“Yeah, okay.”

They left around seven thirty. After changing trains a couple of times, they reached Takaosanguchi just before nine. Hikers with walking poles and backpacks were already heading toward the mountain.

“Those people look serious.”

“We're taking the lift.”

“All the way up?”

“Not all the way.”

His friend stopped walking.

“How much farther after that?”

“I'm not sure. We'll see when we get there.”

The chairlift opened at nine. An attendant waved them forward as a two-person seat came around behind them. They sat down without the chair stopping.

A little way up, his friend pushed his phone deep into his pocket.

“I'm not dropping that.”

Jeff looked down through the space between their shoes. The trees were getting farther below them.

“Good idea.”

His friend held tightly to the side of the seat.

“Man, it's higher than I thought.”

“Pretty nice view, though.”

“Yeah. Just don't make me look straight down.”

“I think they take a photo on the way down,” Jeff said.

“Of us?”

“Yeah.”

“Okay, we need one of those.”

At the top, they took a paved path under the trees. The shade was cool, but the sun warmed the open stretches.

They passed an entrance with pictures of monkeys on a sign.

“Hold on. There's a monkey park up here?”

“Yeah.”

“On a mountain?”

“Apparently.”

“Have you been?”

“No. The guy I came with last time didn't go inside.”

“Can we go?”

“On the way back. Let's get to the temple first.”

His friend gave the sign another look before following Jeff.

Farther along, a huge cedar stood beside the path. Thick roots twisted around its trunk and rose above the ground.

Jeff stopped next to it.

“I remember this one.”

“What is it?”

“Look at the roots.”

His friend stepped closer.

“Those look like tentacles.”

“Right? It's called Tako-sugi. Octopus Cedar.”

“That's a pretty good name.”

He walked around the tree to see the roots from the other side. Jeff waited while he took a picture, and then they continued.

Red lights shaped like lanterns stood between the trees along the path. His friend slowed down beside one.

“Think these come on at night?”

“Maybe.”

“Bet that looks cool.”

Soon the path divided. Stone steps climbed to the left, while a gentler trail curved to the right.

“Which way?”

Jeff studied the steps.

“This way, I think.”

“You think?”

“I've taken these stairs before. That part I remember.”

“Very reassuring.”

“I was following a guy from work. He knew where he was going.”

His friend leaned back to look up the staircase.

“More stairs. Great.”

Jeff set his foot on the first step.

“Race you to the top.”

Before Jeff could finish his challenge, his friend was already running up the steps.

“Hey, wait!”

Jeff chased after him. His friend was quick at first but slowed halfway up, and Jeff caught him and passed him.

“Come on!” Jeff called.

“I'm coming!”

At the top, Jeff stopped with his hands on his hips, struggling to catch his breath. His friend reached him a moment later.

“No more racing,” he said.

“You started early.”

“You challenged me.”

They waited until they could breathe normally, then started walking again.

Tall wooden boards lined the next part of the path, covered in long columns of black Japanese characters. Both of them slowed to look.

“Whoa. That's a lot of writing.”

“Yeah.”

“What does all that say? Rules for the mountain?”

Jeff examined one board. He recognized a few characters, but that wasn't enough to understand the writing.

“Couldn't tell you.”

More boards stood farther along the path.

“That's a lot of rules.”

“If they're rules.”

They walked on until a large gate appeared, with wooden buildings beyond it.

His friend stopped to take a picture.

“There's a shrine all the way up here?”

“A temple, I think. Yakuoin.”

“How do you know?”

“That's what the guy from work called it.”

Inside the gate, his friend kept pausing to photograph the buildings. Jeff waited near the place where visitors were praying. When his friend caught up, they watched the people ahead of them before stepping forward to pay their respects.

As they left, Jeff glanced up the path that continued toward the summit.

“The summit's another twenty minutes or so. Want to go?”

“You're serious?”

“We're pretty close.”

“I flew here yesterday. You made me run up those stairs. I'm good.”

“Okay. We can head down.”

“Now you're talking.”

They took their time walking back. When they reached the monkey park, Jeff followed his friend straight inside.

Inside, a young monkey kept bothering a larger one. Each time it was chased away, it waited a few seconds before returning.

His friend watched the enclosure longer than Jeff had expected.

“Look. He's doing it again.”

The larger monkey pushed the little one away again.

“Doesn't take a hint,” Jeff said.

“Nope.”

Near the exit, his friend found a machine selling toys in capsules.

“What's in this?”

“Looks like pins with the monkeys on them.”

He dropped in some coins and turned the handle. A capsule fell into the tray, and inside were two small pins showing monkeys from the park.

He turned one of the pins over in his hand.

“What do you do with these?”

“Put them on your bag.”

“Or yours?”

“You bought them.”

He put them away, still grinning.

They'd been walking for hours by the time they reached the chairlift. His friend sat as soon as a seat came around.

On the way down, Jeff noticed a camera ahead.

“Oh, the picture.”

“Where?”

“Up there.”

A photographer shouted something in Japanese. Jeff's friend gave the camera a thumbs-up, while Jeff turned a second too late.

At the bottom, they spotted their photograph on a display screen.

His friend burst out laughing.

“Dude. Look at your face.”

“I was turning around.”

“What are you even looking at?”

Jeff checked the photograph again. His friend was giving a thumbs-up, but his eyes were closed.

“Your eyes are shut.”

His friend leaned in for a closer look.

“Oh, man.”

He stared at the photograph for another moment.

“I'm getting it.”

“Seriously?”

His friend took out his wallet.

He paid for the photograph. When they handed him the print, he looked at it once more and slipped it into his backpack, careful not to bend it.

# Jeff — Episode 4: Plum Blossoms

As they left the chairlift, his friend checked his backpack to make sure the photo he'd bought hadn't bent. Jeff bought two manju at one shop, rice crackers next door, and a couple of rice balls farther along.

“You’re still hungry?” his friend asked, shifting the backpack on his shoulder. “We just came down a mountain.”

“It's lunch.”

“Where are we eating?”

“Down by the river. Someone at work said the plum trees were blooming.”

“The what trees?”

“Plum trees. I thought we could eat by the flowers. Do a little hanami.”

“You ever done that?”

“Not really.”

His friend looked at the bags Jeff was carrying. “Did you get anything to sit on?”

“No.”

“So how’s this supposed to work?”

“We can eat while we walk.”

“Is that still a picnic?”

“No idea.”

They turned off the main road onto a street between houses. Laundry hung behind low walls, and branches from small gardens reached over the sidewalk. His friend stopped every now and then to take a picture.

“You don't see streets like this from the train,” he said.

“What are you taking pictures of?”

“Houses.”

“They're just houses.”

“Yeah. I know.”

Plum trees lined the path near the river. Most had small white flowers, while others had dark pink ones. Jeff waited as his friend took a close-up picture of a branch.

“They're different colors.”

“Looks like it.”

Jeff unwrapped a manju and handed it to him.

“What's inside?”

“Something sweet. I think it's beans.”

His friend took a bite and looked at the filling.

“Sweet beans?”

“Apparently.”

He tried another bite, then held on to what was left.

“Too sweet for me.”

“I've got rice crackers.”

“What's in those?”

“Rice.”

“More rice.”

Jeff broke a rice cracker in half and gave him a piece. His friend finished his before Jeff could put away the bag.

“Okay. These are good.”

His friend wrapped up the unfinished manju.

Farther along, they stopped beside the river to eat their rice balls. His friend followed the numbers on the plastic wrapper and pulled the thin strip down the middle. The seaweed stuck to one side, leaving him holding a loose sheet of plastic.

“What am I supposed to pull?”

“Here. Give me that.”

Jeff slipped the rest of the plastic out from under the seaweed and folded it around the rice.

“How'd you do that?”

“You have to take the pieces off in order.”

“It's like a magic trick.”

His friend took a bite, including the seaweed, and chewed for a moment.

“I'm not crazy about the black part.”

“The seaweed?”

“Yeah. Can I take it off?”

He tried to peel the seaweed away, but it tore. Bits of rice came with it and stuck to his fingers. Jeff was already peeling the seaweed off his own rice ball.

“I don't really like it either,” Jeff said.

“You could've told me that.”

“You didn't ask.”

Jeff’s rice ball had salmon inside. His friend ate the rice off his fingers and left the torn seaweed on the wrapper.

They finished eating by the river. Flowers covered the branches, though there were hardly any leaves yet. A light breeze shook the paper in his friend’s hand. He wandered over to photograph another tree, with the rest of his rice ball in one hand and his phone in the other.

“Couldn't you finish eating first?” Jeff called.

“Where am I supposed to put it?”

When his friend returned, Jeff held out the bag for the empty wrappers.

They followed the path to a wooden house behind a garden wall. A sign at the gate said *Takao Komagino Garden*. They could see a pond through the entrance, with a house behind it that was larger than either of them expected.

“Can we go in?”

“Let's see.”

Carp swam slowly around the pond. His friend waited near the edge for a large orange-and-white one to come close enough for a photo. Across the water, ropes ran from high above a pine tree down to the branches.

“What's all that string for? Are they tying the tree up?”

“I don't know.”

“You don't know anything.”

“Hang on.”

Jeff found a garden staff member nearby.

「すみません。あの松の縄、何のためですか」

「雪の重みで枝が折れないようにするんですよ」

「ありがとうございます」

He went back and told his friend the ropes were there to protect the branches from the weight of snow.

“Does it snow that much here?”

“I guess sometimes.”

“Some tour guide you are.”

“I've only been here three months.”

They removed their shoes before entering the house. In one room, tea was served at seats facing the garden. His friend took out his phone while they looked over the menu.

“I'm ordering this time.”

“Go ahead.”

He typed into a translation app and quietly practiced the Japanese on the screen.

“Matcha futatsu, yoroshiku onegaishimasu.”

He tried again more slowly, then twice more, before turning the phone face down.

“Okay.”

“Ready?”

He raised his hand as a staff member walked past the room.

「すみません！」

「はい」

His friend pointed to the matcha on the menu.

「抹茶二つ、よろしくお願いします」

「かしこまりました。少々お待ちください」

After the server left, his friend looked at Jeff, who gave him a thumbs-up.

“Perfect.”

“It worked?”

“Two are coming.”

He returned the thumbs-up.

The server brought two bowls of matcha, each with a small sweet. His friend took a sip and pulled the bowl away from his mouth.

“Whoa. That's bitter.”

Jeff tasted his tea. “Yeah, it is.”

His friend ate the sweet and tried another sip of tea.

“Okay. You have to go back and forth.”

“Maybe that's the idea.”

After tea, they went outside and sat by the pond. His friend stretched his legs out in front of him.

“Are we done walking?”

“For a bit.”

A carp appeared from behind a rock, swam just below the surface, and disappeared into the darker water across the pond. Another followed. His friend watched without reaching for his phone.

“Hey, remember that festival we went to when we were, like, ten?”

“Which festival?”

“The one where we went back to your house afterward.”

Jeff kept his eyes on the pond. “Kind of.”

“Your uncle and his wife were there. She made some kind of meat dish.”

“What kind?”

“It was in a pot. Kind of sweet. Man, that was good.”

“Was that Japanese food?”

“I figured it was. She made it.”

Jeff tried to remember what they’d eaten that day, but the name wouldn’t come to him.

“I'll ask my uncle sometime.”

“He lives nearby, right?”

“Sagamihara. The next town over.”

“Tell him I want some more of that.”

“Think he’ll remember?”

“I remember it.”

His friend went back to watching the fish. Someone passed behind them on the gravel path, their footsteps fading toward the house.

“You know,” he said after a while, “back home you wouldn’t have sat in a garden all afternoon.”

“It's quiet.”

“I know.”

“You're not getting up either.”

“My legs won't let me.”

Jeff stayed on the bench. His friend leaned back and was quiet for a while.

It was after three when they finally got up. They had barely started down the road when his friend asked what was next.

“Hot springs,” Jeff said.

“There's more?”

“You get to sit down.”

“Sold.”

The sun was low when they reached the area around Takaosanguchi Station. At a bathhouse nearby, they left their shoes at the entrance and went into the changing room. His friend stopped at the lockers.

“Wait. Where are the swimsuits?”

“There aren't any.”

“We go in naked?”

“Yeah.”

“Everybody?”

“Everybody.”

He looked around. “You could’ve warned me.”

“Would you have come?”

“...Probably.”

He followed Jeff to the washing area, washed, then slowly lowered himself into the bath. The hot water made him lift his shoulders at first, but before long he had both arms resting along the edge.

“Okay,” he said. “This is nice.”

“Told you.”

“My legs feel a lot better.”

“Mine too.”

Later, they ordered beer at the bathhouse restaurant. His friend took a drink and put down his glass.

“That was a pretty good day.”

“Not a bad way to spend one.”

“We should do this every weekend.”

“Who's paying?”

“Was it that expensive?”

“The chairlift, the monkeys, the baths. And now beer.”

His friend looked at his glass.

“Okay, once a month.”

“Even that's pushing it.”

“Man, you're cheap.”

“You can pay next time.”

It was dark when they left. They took the train from Takaosanguchi and changed at Takao for the Chuo Line. His friend closed his eyes the moment he sat down.

Jeff gave his shoulder a nudge as they approached Hachioji.

“Hey. Wake up. We have to change.”

“Huh? Already?”

“Keep sleeping and you'll end up at Tokyo Station.”

His friend opened his eyes.

“Aren't we in Tokyo now?”

“Tokyo Station. Come on.”

The train arrived at Hachioji. They got up and stepped out onto the platform.

# Jeff — Episode 5: The Maze

“Push down right there.”

Jeff leaned on the suitcase lid while his friend worked the zipper around the edge. It stopped halfway, leaving a shirt sleeve sticking through the opening.

“It was closed when you got here,” Jeff said.

“Yeah. Not sure what happened.”

Jeff pushed the sleeve back inside and held the lid down until his friend got the zipper shut. Then they carried the suitcase downstairs together.

His friend was moving to Shinjuku that day. Before leaving the States, he'd found a furnished apartment through the Japanese language school he'd enrolled in. Orientation started at eleven, and he could pick up the keys in the afternoon.

Walking toward Aihara Station, his friend asked, “Hey, what do you usually say when you get to work?”

“At the restaurant? Mostly *otsukaresama desu*. Everyone says it.”

“Ot-su-ka-re-sa-ma-desu.”

“Why are you practicing that?”

“I'm gonna try it at school. Gotta know how to say something.”

They carried on toward the station.

They rode the Yokohama Line to Hashimoto, then changed to the Keio Line. His friend kept the suitcase between his feet and opened the school's email again.

“It says the school's fifteen minutes from the west exit.”

“We should be fine, then.”

“You know where the west exit is?”

“We'll find it.”

They reached Shinjuku shortly after nine.

People spilled onto the platform and headed upstairs. Jeff followed the crowd, but he and his friend stopped before the ticket gates. Exit signs hung overhead in several directions. They spotted one for the west exit and followed it until they reached another junction full of signs.

“I think it's this way.” Jeff went ahead.

His friend pulled the suitcase toward another corridor. “Shouldn't west be over there?”

“That's not where the sign points.”

“All right. Lead the way.”

They walked down an underground passage, climbed a flight of stairs, and stepped onto a street. His friend checked the map on his phone. The blue dot was nowhere near the school; they were on the opposite side of the station.

“Wrong side.”

“Yep.”

“So now what?”

Jeff looked back toward the stairs. “We go down again.”

The underground passages were even busier now. The suitcase wheels kept catching in the lines between the floor tiles. His friend carried it a short distance before setting it down and dragging it again.

“Hey, haven't we passed that guy already?” His friend grabbed Jeff's sleeve.

“Which guy?”

“The one carrying the black bag.”

“Could be someone who looks like him.”

“No, I'm almost sure it's the same guy.”

“Maybe he thinks he's seen us before, too.”

His friend looked around, but the man had vanished into the crowd.

“Seriously. How are we supposed to get out of here?”

The corridor split again. Jeff studied the signs hanging above them.

“This feels like a dungeon in a video game.”

“You turn a corner and there's another hallway.”

“And when you find an exit, it takes you somewhere you don't want to be.”

Jeff stopped a woman walking toward them and asked how to reach the west exit. She pointed back the way she'd come and explained the route. They thanked her and headed off, only to start arguing about her directions almost immediately.

“She pointed right, didn't she?”

“Yeah, but I think she meant farther along.”

“How much farther?”

Jeff didn't know. They stepped against the wall to let the commuters move past.

# Jeff — Episode 7: The Chopstick Sleeve

Before the restaurant opened, Jeff was cutting cabbage.

He sliced the washed cabbage into thin strips and dropped them into a strainer. A few pieces were too large, so he took them out and cut them again. Once the water had drained, he packed the cabbage into containers and put them in the fridge. The coworker behind him had to wait for him to close the door before squeezing past.

「ごめんなさい」

「大丈夫。そこ狭いから」

Jeff returned to the sink. Lettuce was next. He was still slow with the knife, but by opening time he'd filled several containers with vegetables.

As customers came in, the order tickets began to pile up. Jeff took vegetables from the fridge and arranged them on plates with tongs. Between orders, he washed dishes until a Japanese coworker helping out front came into the kitchen.

「ジェフ、手が空いたら三番、片づけてもらえる？」

「はい」

Jeff washed his hands before going out to clear tables.

The table had six seats. He started collecting the empty glasses and small plates, then stopped. Beside every place was a pair of chopsticks resting on a small piece of folded paper. All six were folded the same way.

He moved a chopstick aside and picked up the folded paper. His coworker was wiping down the counter, so Jeff showed it to him.

“Hey, what's this? I've seen these around.”

“Chopstick rests. We don't put any out here, so some customers fold their wrappers into little rests.”

“You're not really supposed to put your chopsticks across a plate, either. It's called *watashibashi*.”

“I do that all the time.”

“Yeah, I catch myself doing it sometimes.”

So that was why customers folded those pieces of paper. Even where you rested your chopsticks mattered in Japan.

Jeff unfolded one edge of the paper and saw the restaurant's name.

“Oh, it's a chopstick wrapper.”

“Yep.”

“I collect the nice ones. I like the different designs.”

“Huh.”

He turned the paper over. Creases covered it.

“How do you fold one of these?”

“No idea. You should've asked whoever made it.”

Two large plates were still on the next table. One was piled with edamame, while the salad on the other was almost untouched. There wasn't a single empty pod.

“They didn't eat any of this.”

“You're right. What a waste.”

“Why'd they order it?”

“Who knows? Maybe they thought they'd eat it.”

His coworker picked up the plate of edamame.

“I would've eaten it.”

“All of it?”

“Yeah. I once had two bowls of katsudon at the airport. My friend had two, too.”

“Yeah. We could barely move afterward.”

“I tried my best.”

“Some places used to pack up whatever you couldn't finish and let you take it home. Here, you either finish it or leave it.”

His coworker carried the glasses back to the kitchen. Jeff followed with the plates and emptied the untouched food into the waste container.

“Want to try folding one?”

“Can I?”

“Sure. It's just one. Can't show you how, though.”

“Thanks.”

“You going to put that in your binder, too?”

“If I can fold it.”

“And if you can't?”

Jeff thought about it.

“Might put it in anyway.”

Back at his apartment, Jeff put the new wrapper on the low table. He took two others with different designs from his collection binder and set them beside it. Then he tried to fold the new wrapper into the shape he'd seen at the restaurant that afternoon.

“Any luck?”

“Not yet.”

“I won't.”

After they hung up, Jeff tried the chopsticks on the folded paper again. This time, both stayed in place.

He leaned back to look at it. One fold slowly came undone, and a single chopstick fell off.

Jeff picked it up and slipped the wrinkled wrapper into an empty pocket of his collection binder.

# Jeff — Episode 8: The Book

On his day off, Jeff took the wrinkled chopstick wrapper out of his collection binder.

The night before, he'd tried again and again to fold it into a chopstick rest. Now that he'd flattened it, he couldn't remember which crease he'd made first. He tried again, but the paper opened before he could even set the chopsticks on it.

He found a video on his phone and paused it to study the steps. The person's fingers covered the part he needed to see. When he skipped back a few seconds, he lost track of what the paper was supposed to look like before the fold.

Jeff closed the video. A book with pictures of each step might be easier.

He decided to look in a bookstore in Hashimoto.

Hashimoto was one stop from Aihara. He left the station, walked into a shopping center, and checked the store directory for the bookstore.

He was searching for origami books when a familiar cover caught his eye in the manga section.

It belonged to a series his friend had read constantly back in the States. The copies here were in Japanese.

There was a sample copy of the first volume on the shelf. Jeff picked it up. He didn't know every kanji, but the drawings helped him follow the story. It was easier to read than he'd expected.

He meant to read only a few pages. Instead, he stayed by the shelf, turning back now and then to check something he'd missed.

When he finally looked at his phone, almost an hour had passed.

He could actually read a good part of it.

He closed the book. Volumes two through ten stood beside it.

Maybe he could send them to his friend.

He put the sample back and gathered all ten volumes into a stack. They were heavier than he expected.

He was on his way to the checkout when he remembered why he'd come to the store.

Still carrying the manga, he checked the section guide and went over to arts and crafts. Some covers showed paper cranes and flowers; others had folded boxes and small decorations. He leafed through several books.

One explained how to fold a chopstick rest.

The finished rest wasn't quite the same shape as the ones on the six-person table at work, but it used the same type of wrapper. A photo showed the result, with diagrams beside it for each step.

He went through the diagrams in order. They seemed simple enough.

Another page showed a small container made from a paper napkin. Jeff hesitated, then added the book to his pile.

At the checkout, he asked to have all ten manga volumes sent to his friend's apartment in Shinjuku. The cashier gave him a shipping form. Jeff wrote down the address, paid for the books and delivery, and left with the origami book in a bag.

There was a Japanese word he didn't recognize: 谷折り.

He checked his phone. *Valley fold.* The page also had 山折り, which his phone translated as *mountain fold*.

He folded the wrapper in half, opened it again, and turned it over to inspect the crease.

Following the book's arrows, he folded the wrong way on the third diagram. Then, as he tried to tuck a corner inside, his finger slipped.

He unfolded the wrapper and tried again.

The old creases were still in the way, but this time he managed to tuck the end into place. The rest stood on the table without falling over. When he laid a pair of disposable chopsticks on top, they stayed put.

He looked at it for a while, then compared it with the picture in the book. His was wider, and one side was slightly crooked. Still, the shape was close.

Jeff opened his collection binder. Inside were wrappers he'd saved from restaurants, some printed with names and others with patterns.

He reached for one, then stopped. It was clean and uncreased.

He put it back in the binder and moved his wrinkled chopstick rest to the edge of the table. Then he opened the book to the next page.

Someone called from the dining room.

「はい」

“I want one too.”

“You bought one last time.”

“Yeah, but that was last time.”

“Okay, let's get two.”

They bought two copies of the same photo. His friend put his in his backpack, and Jeff found a place where his wouldn't bend.

“I might put this up in my room when I get home.”

“What about the old one?”

“That one's going up too.”

“With us looking like that?”

“It's a good picture.”

Outside the chairlift station, Jeff checked the train times on his phone.

“Want to go to the same hot spring as last time?”

“Found another place. Let's try that one.”

“Is it far?”

“We take a train, then walk a bit.”

“No more hills, okay?”

“I think it's flat.”

After the train ride, they walked to the bathhouse. His friend looked around while taking off his shoes.

“Think we'll have the place to ourselves at this hour?”

“Don't count on it.”

Only a few people were at the washing stations when they went in.

“See? Hardly anyone here.”

“Not exactly a private bath.”

After their baths, they had a late lunch at the bathhouse restaurant. His friend finished his cold green tea and glanced at the clock.

“I might go back in.”

“We've got time.”

They rested for a while before going in for another bath.

After their second bath, his friend pointed toward the restaurant.

“I could go for a beer.”

“We just ate there.”

“Just a drink this time.”

They went back to their table and ordered a beer each.

“That felt good,” he said, setting down his glass. “Man, I could live here. I'd come every day.”

“Live at the bathhouse?”

“Near it.”

“You'd come every day?”

“Even just on my days off would be nice.”

He took another drink.

“Wouldn't mind more days like this.”

“You planning to climb a mountain every weekend?”

“The mountain part? Once in a while.”

They stayed at the table after their beers were gone. By the time they left, they'd spent about three hours at the bathhouse, and it was busier than before.

His friend fell asleep almost as soon as he sat down on the train.

As they approached the station where they had to change trains, Jeff tapped his friend on the shoulder.

“Hey, wake up. We have to change here.”

He opened his eyes.

“Are we there?”

“We're changing trains.”

He picked up his backpack, stood, and followed Jeff out.

On Sunday, Jeff was standing in front of the fish tank when his friend woke up.

“Looking at them again?”

“One of the eggs is gone.”

His friend crouched beside him and searched among the plants.

“Maybe it's stuck somewhere else.”

“Could be.”

They couldn't find the egg. Jeff stood, and his friend got up beside him.

“Come on, let's get lunch. I'm starving.”

After his friend packed for the trip home, they set out toward the station.

Just around the corner, a man with a shopping bag called Jeff by name and raised his hand. Jeff passed him on his way to the station most mornings.

「おう、ジェフ。この前はお疲れさん」

「お疲れさまです」

「また祭りも頼むよ」

The man laughed and went on his way.

His friend watched the man go.

“You know that guy?”

“We carried the mikoshi together at the festival.”

“You joined a festival out here, too?”

“Yeah. My shoulder was killing me afterward.”

He gave Jeff a quick pat on the shoulder.

“Worse than the mountain?”

“Way worse.”

Outside the supermarket, the woman who usually worked at the register saw Jeff and called from near the entrance.

「あ、ジェフ！　今日はお友達？」

「はい」

「いつものラーメン、新しいの入ったよ」

「ほんとですか。帰りに見ます」

「またね」

Jeff waved, and she went back inside.

“They even know you at the grocery store?”

“I go there all the time.”

“She even knows what you buy.”

“I buy the same stuff every time.”

Before they reached the main road, someone called Jeff's name from across the street.

「ジェフー！」

It was a regular customer from the izakaya. He raised a hand as he walked, and Jeff waved back.

His friend stopped.

“What are you, famous around here?”

“No. They're just people I see all the time.”

“I've been here two days, and nobody's called my name once.”

“You don't live here.”

“Fair point.”

His friend began walking again, then turned to Jeff.

“So what's for lunch?”

“There's a katsudon place nearby.”

“Katsudon again?”

“We can go somewhere else.”

“No, katsudon's fine. Just one bowl this time.”

“One's enough for me too.”

“Wasn't that the plan last time, too?”

Jeff laughed and pointed farther down the street.

“This way. Just a little farther.”

“After all that walking yesterday?”

“It's close.”

His friend adjusted the strap on his backpack and followed Jeff.
