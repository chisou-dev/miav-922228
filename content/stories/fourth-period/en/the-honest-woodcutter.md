1995.
Age 10.
A classroom.
Fourth period.
Moral Education.

The teacher opened her textbook.

"Today we're reading The Honest Woodcutter."

A few hands went up.

"I know this one."

She smiled.

"Some of you probably do.
Some of you probably don't."

The room settled.

"Long ago, there was a poor woodcutter.
Every day he went into the forest with the same iron axe.
One afternoon, the axe slipped from his hands and disappeared into a lake.
He didn't know what to do.
Then a god appeared.
'Is this your golden axe?'
'No.'
'Then perhaps this silver one?'
'No.'
'Mine was just an ordinary iron axe.'
Because he told the truth, the god returned his iron axe — and gave him the gold and silver ones as well."

She turned to the blackboard and wrote:

The Honest Woodcutter

Then she faced the class.

"So…
what do you think this story is trying to teach us?"

Hands shot into the air.

"Don't lie."

"Be honest."

"Don't be greedy."

The teacher nodded after each answer.

From the back of the room,
another hand went up.

"But…"

A boy stood.

"If the god already knew whose axe it was…
why didn't he just give it back?"

The room burst into laughter.

The teacher laughed too.

"That's one way to look at it."

The bell rang.

Chairs scraped across the floor.

Books snapped shut.

Fourth period was over.

Thirty years later.

He was forty.

He owned a small company.

Eight employees.

Nothing remarkable.

No fancy office.

No impressive website.

Just steady work.

Every morning,
he unlocked the door before anyone else arrived.

He swept the floor.

Wiped the desks.

Started the kettle.

No one had ever asked him to.

He simply liked arriving before the day began.

He returned every phone call.

He showed up five minutes early.

If he promised Friday,
he meant Friday.

They were small things.

Almost invisible.

Yet year after year,
the company grew.

One client recommended him to another.

Then another.

He had never been good at selling himself.

His business grew because other people talked about him when he wasn't in the room.

If someone had known him at fifteen,
they wouldn't have believed it.

Back then,
he skipped school.

Argued with teachers.

Spent his nights with the same group of boys who believed being feared was the same thing as being respected.

He left school early.

Started working while he was still a teenager.

His first boss yelled almost every day.

"Answer me."

"Do it again."

"Wrong."

Years earlier,
he would've yelled back.

Instead,
he kept his mouth shut.

He apologized.

He came back the next morning.

And the morning after that.

Eventually,
the shouting stopped.

People started handing him more responsibility.

It took him years to understand something no one had ever taught him.

Being feared gets you noticed.

Being trusted keeps people coming back.

One afternoon,
his phone rang.

It was someone he hadn't heard from in years.

An old friend.

Fresh out of prison.

"I need a job."

Everyone told him the same thing.

"Don't do it."

He almost listened.

But he couldn't.

Once,
they had believed in the same things.

So he hired him.

At first,
everything went well.

He showed up early.

Worked hard.

Never complained.

For the first time,
he thought
maybe people really could change.

The first few months went smoothly.

His old friend arrived before everyone else.

He learned quickly.

He laughed often.

Customers liked him.

The others in the office started relaxing.

Maybe everyone had been wrong.

Maybe prison didn't have to define a person's life.

One afternoon,
a client called.

"There seems to be a problem."

He listened without interrupting.

"The envelope was short."

He frowned.

"How much?"

"A little."

There was a pause.

"Twenty thousand yen."

It wasn't much money.

Not enough to hurt the company.

Not enough to ruin anyone's life.

But that wasn't what bothered him.

The cash had been counted.

Sealed.

Hand-delivered.

Someone had opened the envelope.

He already knew who it was.

He took the money from his own wallet.

Drove to the client's office.

"I'm sorry."

That was all he said.

The client accepted the envelope.

Neither of them mentioned it again.

That night,
the office was quiet.

Only the two of them remained.

"Why?"

His friend said nothing.

Silence settled between them.

After a while,
his friend spoke.

"It was only twenty thousand yen."

He said nothing.

His friend leaned back in his chair.

"You've changed."

A faint smile crossed his face.

"The old you would've laughed."

The silence remained.

His friend stood.

He stopped at the door.

One hand rested on the knob.

"I thought it through."

A brief pause.

"I made sure no one would notice."

The door clicked shut.

The office was silent again.

An empty envelope lay on the desk beneath the light.

He looked at it for a long time.

No client would ever know.

No employee would ever know.

He would.

Outside,
traffic kept moving,
without ever stopping.
